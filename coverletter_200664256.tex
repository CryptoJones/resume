What draws me to this role is that exposing capabilities to an AI assistant through secure, well-defined interfaces is exactly what I build. My open-source Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers do for AI agents what App Intents and SiriKit do for Siri --- describe a capability's schema, validate every invocation, and give the model a safe, structured way to act on the user's behalf. I work daily with tool-use / function calling, multi-provider LLM routing, and RAG, and I recently landed three merged security fixes to \href{https://github.com/omnigent-ai/omnigent}{Omnigent}, an open-source meta-harness for AI agents, hardening input validation, authentication, and policy enforcement around exactly this kind of tool-calling surface. It is real code you can read today at \href{https://github.com/CryptoJones}{github.com/CryptoJones}. I watched this year's WWDC sessions closely, and the Foundation Models framework's new Language Model protocol --- one API you can swap Apple's own model, Claude, or Gemini behind, with Dynamic Profiles to switch on the fly --- is exactly the pluggable, multi-provider model routing I already build into my own agents; getting to contribute to that inside Siri is what excites me most about this role.

Just as important, the way I build maps to how Apple builds. I run inference locally and privately on my own hardware rather than shipping user data to a third-party API, and I harden every interface I expose --- my MCP servers follow NSA security guidance, with per-invocation guards and audit logging. That privacy-first, security-conscious instinct, plus 25+ years of shipping production software and a habit of clear technical writing, is what I would bring to making Siri integrations that feel reliable and trustworthy. I am a fast study in a new stack --- I have shipped across C\#/.NET, Python, TypeScript, and Java --- and I am eager to go deep on Swift and the App Intents framework.
